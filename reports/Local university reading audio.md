# Speak university files without rewriting them

A student with **24 GB of RAM and 2 GB of VRAM** can turn university `.md`, `.txt`, and born-digital `.pdf` files into local audio, with no GPU upgrade, by extracting the words on the machine and speaking them with **upstream Kokoro-82M on CPU**. Sources fetched on **15 November 2026** put that 82-million-parameter model at roughly **1.2–1.8 GB of process RAM** and, on four CPU threads, at a real-time factor of **0.17–0.27**, inside a budget of **30 seconds of processing per 1 KB of text**. The 2 GB GPU stays idle: no Kokoro run on a 2 GB card was found, and the published peaks come from 12–32 GB cards. **Piper medium** is the fallback when a one-thread timing run misses the budget, because its measured real-time factor on one thread is **0.054**. Pocket-tts also fits and is fast, and no listening score for it was found. Qwen3-TTS is the cloud product in the same survey; its local 0.6B checkpoint measured **2.58 GB of VRAM** and **0.26× realtime on CPU**, so it misses both the card and the clock. These engines phonemize the text they are given. NotebookLM Audio Overview does the other job: it is a hosted discussion of uploaded sources.

## Three engines fit this PC, and they are not three Kokoro skins

The five GitHub projects named as starting points were read on 15 November 2026. Four of them synthesize **Kokoro-82M** from hexgrad, and the fifth, LocalText2Voice, is a desktop app that merely includes Kokoro among other engines. They are interfaces around one model, or multi-engine applications, so they do not count as separate answers. The distinct local readers that fit 24 GB of RAM and a 2 GB GPU left unused are **Piper**, **upstream Kokoro-82M**, and **Kyutai pocket-tts**. **Qwen3-TTS on DashScope** is the cloud path whose official local weights do not fit this machine. espeak-ng is the formant floor under Piper, and sherpa-onnx is a runtime for Piper and Kokoro, not a fourth voice. StyleTTS 2 still measures small, and it is the June 2023 architecture Kokoro already ships as a maintained decoder.

### Piper is the CPU pipeline with a wide speed margin

Piper is a VITS voice that turns phonemes into a waveform. The installable tree in September 2026 is `OHF-Voice/piper1-gpl`. `pip install piper-tts` is the documented install, and PyPI `piper-tts` **1.8.0**, uploaded **4 September 2026**, is declared **GPL-3.0-or-later**. The old `rhasspy/piper` repository is archived. Voices are an `.onnx` file plus `.onnx.json`. The Python API and the CLI take a plain string and write WAV; `python3 -m piper -m <voice> -f test.wav` is the documented command. `length_scale` on `SynthesisConfig` changes duration inside the model. The engine samples noise, so the same sentence is not bit-identical across runs. In `piper-tts`, espeak-ng is the phonemizer. sherpa-onnx can run the same ONNX voices without Python, and since sherpa-onnx v2.0.0 that path ignores Piper’s `data_dir` and no longer uses espeak-ng, so abbreviation pronunciation there is the lexicon’s and was not compared with `piper-tts`. ([OHF-Voice/piper1-gpl](https://github.com/OHF-Voice/piper1-gpl), [piper-tts on PyPI](https://pypi.org/project/piper-tts/), [sherpa-onnx TTS](https://k2-fsa.github.io/sherpa/onnx/tts/))

Piper itself does not open `.md`, `.txt`, or `.pdf`. It speaks a string. Existing local tools add the files unevenly. QuickPiperAudiobook is a one-command Piper converter for PDF, EPUB, TXT, HTML, and other formats Calibre’s `ebook-convert` can read, and it describes conversion as offline, with no LLM rewrite in the README. make-audiobook accepts EPUB, PDF, DOCX, TXT, Markdown, and HTML and can use Piper or Kokoro, with a CLI and a GUI, and it does not describe a summarizer. audiobook-generator turns PDF, EPUB, and TXT into WAV or MP3 with Piper and documents abbreviation expansion, which is pronunciation cleanup. Calibre’s PDF extraction, which QuickPiperAudiobook depends on, was not compared with PyMuPDF on a two-column paper. ([QuickPiperAudiobook](https://github.com/C-Loftus/QuickPiperAudiobook), [make-audiobook](https://github.com/tigger-developer/make-audiobook), [audiobook-generator](https://github.com/marcusrprojects/audiobook-generator))

Hardware is a CPU problem of a few hundred megabytes. On 4 November 2026, Piper medium `lessac` was **15.7 million parameters**, a **63 MB** ONNX file, and about **200 MB** of extra process RAM; Piper high `ljspeech` was **28.3 million**, **114 MB**, and about **270 MB**. On 5 November 2026, on an Intel Core i9-13900K, peak RSS including Python was **503 MB** for medium and **639 MB** for high. A separate bench fetched 15 November 2026 lists Piper CPU RAM at **470 MB**. No GPU is required. One CUDA measurement used **526 MB**. Official VRAM is not stated. **24 GB of system RAM is far above every Piper figure found.** ([Piper vs Kokoro, 4 November 2026](https://bthek1.github.io/DL_tasks/Frameworks/piper_vs_kokoro.html), [VoxRT, 5 November 2026](https://huggingface.co/blog/VoxRT/small-cpu-text-to-speech-models-compared-piper-kok), [tts-bench](https://5uck1ess.github.io/tts-bench/speed.html))

Voice quality for an hour of prose was not measured with a listener score. No MOS, arena row, or lecture test for Piper was found. The evidence in hand is a round-trip word-error rate with Whisper, 20 sentences times three passes: **3.07%** for medium and **3.27%** for high, against **1.23%** for Kokoro. The espeak front end read `$3.50` as “dollar three point five zero,” turned “St.” into something Whisper heard as “cent” on the Kokoro-with-espeak path, and said the past-tense verb “read” as “reed.” A qualitative CPU guide ranks Kokoro fp32 as the most natural of a small set that includes Piper and espeak-ng. espeak-ng itself is formant synthesis, GPL-3.0-or-later, a few megabytes, with release **1.52.0** listed as newest and master commits on **3 November 2026**. It will read a book and it will sound like a formant screen reader. No espeak-ng real-time factor was published, so none is stated here. ([Piper vs Kokoro, 4 November 2026](https://bthek1.github.io/DL_tasks/Frameworks/piper_vs_kokoro.html), [espeak-ng](https://github.com/espeak-ng/espeak-ng/), [Big Iron](https://www.bigiron.cc/guides/speech-on-a-cpu-without-avx-piper-kokoro-whisper-and-why-int8-got-slower))

Speed is where Piper medium wins. Real-time factor is synthesis time divided by audio length. On the 4 November 2026 four-vCPU machine, medium was **0.054** on one thread and **0.032** on four; high was **0.433** and **0.269**. On the 5 November i9, medium was **5.8%** and **2.1%** of realtime; high was **42.5%** and **12.4%**. Kokoro’s own card, in a note it dates April 2025, says about **1,000 characters** become about **one minute** of audio, so 30 seconds per 1 KB is about real-time factor **0.5** if a kilobyte is about a thousand characters. A stricter planning band, from about **155 words per minute** and an assumed **170–205 English words per kilobyte**, is about **0.38–0.45**. Piper medium is several times under either bar on one strong thread. Piper high on one thread is close to the line and misses the **0.38** band before PDF parsing is counted. tts-bench lists Piper at **58.83×** realtime; clip length and thread count on that page are not stated, so that figure is not converted into seconds per kilobyte. Cost is local and unpaid. Privacy holds after the voice file is on disk: the CPU rows in the November benchmark used **0 MB** of GPU memory, and the CLI takes a filesystem path. Whether the package phones home for anything other than the voice download was not traced. ([hexgrad/Kokoro-82M](https://huggingface.co/hexgrad/Kokoro-82M), [Piper vs Kokoro, 4 November 2026](https://bthek1.github.io/DL_tasks/Frameworks/piper_vs_kokoro.html), [VoxRT, 5 November 2026](https://huggingface.co/blog/VoxRT/small-cpu-text-to-speech-models-compared-piper-kok))

The licence is the part that is easy to misread. The engine is **GPL-3.0-or-later**. Each voice has its own data licence. LJ Speech, the `ljspeech` voice, is public domain. Blizzard 2013 Lessac, the usual fast demo voice, is under a research licence and commercial use is unclear. `ryan` is CC BY-NC-SA 4.0. `VOICES.md` says Piper is intended for personal use and research and that some voices may have restrictive licences. That sentence is the project’s intent; it does not replace the GPL. Personal listening on one machine is a local run. Shipping the binary inside an app is a distribution question the GPL actually governs. A 4 November 2026 page also says Piper is looking for maintainers. ([Piper VOICES.md](https://github.com/OHF-Voice/piper1-gpl/blob/main/docs/VOICES.md), [Piper vs Kokoro, 4 November 2026](https://bthek1.github.io/DL_tasks/Frameworks/piper_vs_kokoro.html))

### Upstream Kokoro is the natural small voice, spoken from the words it is given

Kokoro-82M v1.0 was published **27 January 2025**: **82 million parameters**, **8 languages**, **54 voices**, Apache-licensed weights, a StyleTTS 2 decoder with ISTFTNet, decoder only, no diffusion. The card’s install is `pip install kokoro` with `soundfile` and system **espeak-ng**, grapheme-to-phoneme through **misaki**, output **24 kHz WAV**. The code repository `hexgrad/kokoro` is Apache-2.0; its last recorded push is **6 August 2025**. No newer weight row after v1.0 appears on that card. The card publishes no RAM, no VRAM, no real-time factor, and no MOS. Its quality sentence is “comparable quality to larger models,” with no named cloud product and no NotebookLM comparison. The official example passes a string into `KPipeline` and yields graphemes, phonemes, and audio. An inline IPA override such as `[Kokoro](/kˈOkəɹO/)` changes pronunciation of a token. It does not paraphrase the sentence. ([hexgrad/Kokoro-82M](https://huggingface.co/hexgrad/Kokoro-82M), [hexgrad/kokoro](https://github.com/hexgrad/kokoro/))

The five wrappers, checked the same day, do not change that contract. **audio.cpp** (`0xShug0/audio.cpp`) is a C++ ggml engine, Apache-2.0 in its `LICENSE` file, pushed **15 November 2026**, with a CLI and a local web UI. Kokoro is one of 148 families, loaded as GGUF, and the documented Kokoro call is a `--text` string, not a `.md`, `.txt`, or `.pdf` path. **PierrunoYT/Kokoro-TTS-Local** is Apache-2.0, last commit **7 October 2026**, an interactive CLI plus a Gradio page, and it speaks the sentence the user types. **hangry-labs/kokoroTTS** is an Apache-2.0 fork of hexgrad/kokoro, last commit **28 October 2026**, Docker browser UI and HTTP API, text or opt-in SSML, and it returns an error if an OpenAI-style `instructions` field is filled, because Kokoro does not support natural-language instructions. **nazdridoy/kokoro-tts** is MIT, CLI only, last commit and release **22 August 2026**, and it accepts `.txt`, `.epub`, `.pdf`, and stdin. `.md` is not listed. **LocalText2Voice** is MIT, last commit **7 November 2026**, and it imports `.txt`, `.md`, `.docx`, and DRM-free `.epub`. PDF is not in that list. Its README says optional normalization prepares a pronunciation-friendly copy without changing the source text, and its “podcast” output is narration plus music. An LLM script generator is still a roadmap item. None of the five document all three of `.md`, `.txt`, and `.pdf`. None document an LLM that summarizes the source. ([audio.cpp Kokoro doc](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/0xShug0/audio.cpp/main/docs/models/kokoro_tts.md), [PierrunoYT/Kokoro-TTS-Local](https://github.com/PierrunoYT/Kokoro-TTS-Local), [hangry-labs/kokoroTTS](https://github.com/hangry-labs/kokoroTTS), [nazdridoy/kokoro-tts](https://github.com/nazdridoy/kokoro-tts), [LocalText2Voice](https://github.com/estebanstifli/localtext2voice))

Measured memory fits this PC on CPU and is unproven on the 2 GB GPU. On 4 November 2026, fp32 ONNX was **326 MB** of weights and about **500–600 MB** of extra RAM; int8 was a **92 MB** file and about **350 MB** extra. On 5 November, peak RSS for fp32 was **1,246 MB**. tts-bench, fetched 15 November 2026, lists Kokoro CPU RAM **1.82 GB** and CUDA VRAM **925 MB** on an RTX 5090. A separate RTX 5080 write-up reports about **1.1 GB** allocated by PyTorch and a whole-GPU nvidia-smi peak of about **2.6 GB**, of which about **0.9 GB** was already in use. Hangry-labs, on 3 October 2026, measured standard Kokoro weights at **317.6 MiB** allocated before inference; the **2,052 MiB** reserved peak is after German and Vietnamese checkpoints were also loaded. **24 GB of RAM covers every Kokoro process-RAM figure found**, about **0.7–2.5 GB**. A 2 GB GPU contains the 925 MB and 1.1 GB allocation figures and does not contain the 2.6 GB whole-GPU reading or the multi-checkpoint reservation. Those readings were taken on 16–32 GB cards. **No source states a test on a 2 GB GPU.** CPU execution is the path that does not need the upgrade: the official library, the ONNX package, audio.cpp `--backend cpu`, and the wrappers all document a CPU route. ([Piper vs Kokoro, 4 November 2026](https://bthek1.github.io/DL_tasks/Frameworks/piper_vs_kokoro.html), [tts-bench](https://5uck1ess.github.io/tts-bench/speed.html), [VRAM16](https://vram16.ullu-egg.com/en/models/kokoro-82m/), [hangry-labs VRAM benchmark](https://hangry-labs.github.io/kokoroTTS/benchmarks/vram/VRAM_BENCHMARKS.html))

Naturalness sits with other small open models and below the top closed voices, on short-clip votes, not on textbooks. A Hugging Face TTS Arena snapshot loaded on **10 October 2026** lists Kokoro v1.0 at **1478 ±22** (about 1,000 votes, marked retired on that board) and Chatterbox at **1481 ±19**. ElevenLabs Eleven v3 was **1501 ±34**. The Kokoro and Chatterbox error bars overlap. A **7 November 2026** write-up of an older arena lists Kokoro at **1465** and Chatterbox at **1468**, 29th of 41. The same day’s Artificial Analysis board is cited as Kokoro-82M Elo **1064**, rank **63 of 104**, against Eleven v4 at **1339**. Arena scores move, and they are preference votes on short clips. Using them as a lecture ranking is an extrapolation. No public MOS on papers or multi-hour stability was found. misaki is the better front end of the two that were tested: it said “three dollars and fifty cents” where espeak said “dollar three point five zero,” and it handled the verb “read,” and it still failed “St.” PierrunoYT’s A-to-F grades are training-data grades, with `af_heart` at A. They are not a cloud listening test. ([Yaps, 10 October 2026](https://www.yaps.ai/blog/best-local-tts-models), [gpuwerk, 7 November 2026](https://gpuwerk.com/blog/open-source-tts-models/), [Piper vs Kokoro, 4 November 2026](https://bthek1.github.io/DL_tasks/Frameworks/piper_vs_kokoro.html))

Speed clears the 30-second budget when several threads are used, and it sits on the line when one strong thread is handed a whole kilobyte. On 4 November 2026, fp32 real-time factor was **0.476** on one thread and **0.267** on four; int8 was **0.359** and **0.234**; CUDA was about **0.03**. On 5 November, fp32 was **51.7%** of realtime on one i9 thread and **17.2%** on four. Combined with the April 2025 rule of about one minute of audio per thousand characters, that one pinned i9 thread is about **31 seconds** per kilobyte, and those four i9 threads are about **10 seconds**. The four-vCPU result of **0.267** is a different machine and was not converted to seconds in the notes. An 8-vCPU Xeon measurement on **21 October 2026** found fp32 real-time factor **0.1234**. A guide that splits old and new CPUs by AVX reports fp32 at **2.77×** realtime with AVX2 and **0.42×** without it. The no-AVX case would miss the bar, at about **143 seconds** per minute of audio under the same character rule. Whether this PC has AVX2 is not stated. tts-bench’s **14.38×** CPU figure and hangry-labs’ **15.60×** figure have no published clip length here, so they are not turned into seconds per kilobyte. The April 2025 duration rule is older than the November 2026 benches. A laptop-class CPU was not in the cited tests. ([Piper vs Kokoro, 4 November 2026](https://bthek1.github.io/DL_tasks/Frameworks/piper_vs_kokoro.html), [VoxRT, 5 November 2026](https://huggingface.co/blog/VoxRT/small-cpu-text-to-speech-models-compared-piper-kok), [OpenVoiceIndex, 21 October 2026](https://openvoiceindex.com/models/kokoro-82m/on/x86-cpu/), [Big Iron](https://www.bigiron.cc/guides/speech-on-a-cpu-without-avx-piper-kokoro-whisper-and-why-int8-got-slower))

Cost of the local weights is zero at synthesis time. The card’s April 2025 note prices hosted Kokoro under **$1 per million characters** at Replicate (**65 cents**) and DeepInfra (**80 cents**). Those hosts upload the text. They are other companies reselling the Apache model, not a fallback mode the student needs. Privacy of the local path is the weights on disk and an ONNX `InferenceSession` or the PyTorch pipeline. Training-data notes on the card list permissive or non-copyrighted data plus short CC BY corpora. Personal university listening is the use the Apache licence already allows. ([hexgrad/Kokoro-82M](https://huggingface.co/hexgrad/Kokoro-82M))

### Pocket-tts is the other local neural model; Qwen’s API is the cloud path that does not fall back onto this GPU

Pocket-tts, as measured on 5 November 2026 in package **4.0.1**, is a **100-million-parameter** autoregressive model conditioned on voice prompts, with **7 languages** in that package. Weights are **CC BY 4.0**, so attribution applies, and the Hub download is gated. It is described as text-to-speech, not as a chatbot. Peak RSS on the i9 test was **968 MB**. tts-bench’s pocket-tts v4 row lists **2.01 GB** of CPU RAM. The two figures were not reconciled; version labels, thread counts, and what “peak” includes differ, and that explanation was not proven. No GPU was in the i9 test. Real-time factor was **23.8%** on two cores, which is under both the **0.5** and the **0.38** reading of the speed bar. It emitted a chunk every **80 ms**. tts-bench lists **3.98×** realtime, again without a clip length, so that number stays unconverted. No MOS was found. Maximum input length is not stated. No document reader for `.md`, `.txt`, or `.pdf` appears in the sources. Streaming every 80 ms does not say how long a chapter may be. ([VoxRT, 5 November 2026](https://huggingface.co/blog/VoxRT/small-cpu-text-to-speech-models-compared-piper-kok), [tts-bench](https://5uck1ess.github.io/tts-bench/speed.html))

KittenTTS is the nearby Apache ONNX line, not a second write-up of pocket-tts. The lightweight models are **15–80 million** parameters and documented as CPU, with no GPU required. tts-bench lists KittenTTS Nano 0.8 at **15 million** parameters, February 2026, **537 MB** CPU RAM, and **31.41×** realtime on the 16-core desktop, with thread count not stated. The same repository’s KittenTTS 2 flagship is **1.7 billion** parameters and is the wrong checkpoint for this PC. A separate weights-licence file was not read; the repo is the source to re-check before any commercial ship. No listener score was found. ([KittenML/KittenTTS](https://github.com/KittenML/KittenTTS), [tts-bench](https://5uck1ess.github.io/tts-bench/speed.html))

Qwen3-TTS, released **22 January 2026**, is the real cloud product with a same-family local checkpoint. Weights are **0.6B and 1.7B**, **Apache-2.0**, installed with `pip install -U qwen-tts`, or called through Alibaba DashScope. A plain synthesis request sends `input.text`, the voice, the language, and optional style instructions to an Alibaba endpoint. The documented maximum is **600 characters** per non-realtime request. The response is an audio URL valid for **24 hours**, or base64 audio if streaming. `output.text` is documented as always null. `optimize_instructions` enhances the instructions field when that flag is set. It is not documented as rewriting `text`. Local `generate_custom_voice` takes `text` plus an optional style `instruct`. Empty `instruct` is the verbatim path. Style text can still change delivery. DashScope data-retention terms were not read. ([QwenLM/Qwen3-TTS](https://github.com/QwenLM/Qwen3-TTS/), [Qwen-TTS API](https://docs.qwencloud.com/api-reference/speech-synthesis/qwen-tts))

The local fallback misses this hardware. tts-bench measured Qwen3-TTS 0.6B CustomVoice at **2.58 GB** of VRAM and **5.19 GB** of CPU RAM, and at **0.26×** realtime on a Ryzen 9 9950X3D. The 1.7B Base row is **4.64 GB** of VRAM. CUDA on an RTX 5090 was **0.63×** for that 0.6B CustomVoice row, still slower than realtime. **24 GB of RAM would hold the 5.19 GB CPU footprint. The 2 GB GPU does not hold 2.58 GB, and the CPU rate misses 30 seconds per kilobyte by a wide margin on a faster desktop than the cited one-thread Kokoro tests.** Qwen’s long-speech word-error figures, about **1.2–2.8%** on its own English long-speech set for 1.7B variants, show that words can survive. They are not a listener score, and that model does not fit 2 GB. A UTMOS of **4.16** on the Qwen page is a speech-tokenizer reconstruction score, not a MOS of a book reading. Price of DashScope is not stated in the notes. What leaves the machine on a plain read is the text, the voice name, the language, any style string, and the API key. ([tts-bench](https://5uck1ess.github.io/tts-bench/speed.html), [QwenLM/Qwen3-TTS](https://github.com/QwenLM/Qwen3-TTS/))

## Kokoro on CPU is the balance; the others lose on quality, certainty, or privacy

The recommendation is **upstream Kokoro-82M, on CPU, sentence by sentence**, using the Apache-2.0 weights at `hexgrad/Kokoro-82M` and the Apache-2.0 library `hexgrad/kokoro` (`pip install kokoro`, with system espeak-ng). The interface is a **CLI**: the model card’s `KPipeline` loop for extracted text, or **nazdridoy/kokoro-tts** (MIT, `kokoro-tts <file>`) when the input is already `.txt` or `.pdf` and the student accepts that tool’s missing Markdown support and its open pronunciation bugs. A small web UI is optional and is not required. PierrunoYT’s Gradio page, hangry-labs’ Docker UI, and audio.cpp’s server UI all exist, and all of them speak a text field. They do not replace extraction.

This is the balance the measurements support. Among engines that fit in 24 GB and do not need the 2 GB GPU, Kokoro is the one with both a listener-board placement and a lower recognition error than Piper, and with a four-thread real-time factor (**0.172** on the i9, **0.267** on the four-vCPU box) that sits under the **0.38–0.50** readings of the speed bar. The text stays on the machine after the weights are downloaded. Personal listening does not require a hosted key.

Piper loses the recommendation on voice, and it wins it back the moment a timing run fails. Medium `lessac` is much faster, and its one-thread margin can absorb a slower CPU than the i9 that was tested. Kokoro’s one-thread result cannot: **0.517** is already just over 30 seconds per minute of audio on a desktop performance core. Piper’s published word-error is higher, it has no arena row, and the fast voice’s dataset licence is the murky one. The public-domain `ljspeech` high voice is the cleaner data licence and, at **0.433** on one thread, has little headroom before extraction. Piper remains the installed fallback, not the default voice. The GPL matters when someone ships an application. It does not block a student pressing play on their own machine.

Pocket-tts loses because the quality evidence is absent, the memory evidence disagrees with itself, the maximum text length is not stated, and nothing in the notes turns a course PDF into pocket-tts audio. KittenTTS nano loses on the same gap: it is fast and small on one bench, and no listening test and no separate weights licence were fetched. Qwen loses on the constraints that define the question. A DashScope read uploads every chunk. The local 0.6B model measured over 2 GB of VRAM and well under realtime on a 16-core desktop. Chatterbox Turbo measured **3.01 GB** of VRAM. Its Nano “3× realtime on 8 cores” claim was not reproduced in the benches fetched, and the project was retired in August 2026. XTTS-v2, F5-TTS, Fish Speech, Orpheus, and Parler-TTS miss the licence, the VRAM, or the CPU clock. They are the wrong tools before taste enters into it.

The wrappers lose as the product to install in place of a pipeline. They speak Kokoro, or they speak many models of which Kokoro is one. audio.cpp’s only Kokoro wall-clock figures in the model doc are on an RK3588, real-time factor **1.415** down to **0.867** on a 185-character Russian clip, which misses a 30-second budget for a minute of audio. That board is not this PC. LocalText2Voice’s own “recommended” profile asks for an **8 GB** NVIDIA GPU. nazdridoy is the useful file CLI, and its GUI is still an open TODO, its last commit is **22 August 2026**, and open issues include Roman numerals and the program inserting the words “Japanese Letter” and “Chinese Letter.” Use that CLI as a speaker of already plain text. Do not treat it as the layout policy.

## Page furniture, a 510-token cap, and a 2 GB card nobody timed

**Two-column pages come out in the wrong order if the extractor only sorts boxes.** PyMuPDF’s own FAQ says `page.get_text(sort=True)` sorts blocks top-left to bottom-right, helps multi-column pages, and is not a full solution. pypdf’s `extraction_mode="layout"` kept two columns on the same line in a project discussion. Unstructured’s `hi_res` docs say that mode has difficulty ordering multi-column pages. OpenDataLoader’s reading-order scores, fetched with the November notes, put simple pages in a band where order is largely solved and show the remaining gaps on multi-column pages, mixed tables, and nested sections. The mitigation is to read born-digital pages with PyMuPDF or pdfminer.six first, keep word positions, and send a file on only when median lines are very short or words cluster in two horizontal positions. The retry path is Docling’s standard CPU pipeline, with artifacts already downloaded and remote services left off, or Marker with `--disable_ocr` or the CPU fast path. Marker’s balanced mode starts a vision-language model. No VRAM figure for that Surya model was found, so treating balanced mode as unsafe on 2 GB is an inference from Marker’s own server requirement and from Docling’s published sizes, not a crash log from a 2 GB card. Docling’s EasyOCR GPU figure is **3.6 GB**. Its formula model is **15.5 GB** of CPU RAM or **21 GB** of GPU memory. Neither belongs in the default path. ([PyMuPDF FAQ](https://github.com/pymupdf/pymupdf/blob/main/docs/faq/index.rst), [pypdf](https://pypdf.readthedocs.io/en/stable/user/extract-text.html), [Unstructured partitioning](https://docs.unstructured.io/open-source/core-functionality/partitioning), [Docling memory sizing](https://github.com/docling-project/docling-serve/issues/234), [Marker README](https://github.com/datalab-to/marker/blob/master/README.md))

**Headers and footers are spoken once per page by a plain text layer, and they sound like a repeated sentence.** PyMuPDF, pypdf, and pdfminer do that. Marker removes headers and footers unless `--keep_pageheader_in_output` and `--keep_pagefooter_in_output` are set, and that removal is still a deletion: a running head can be a section title the model mislabeled. The conservative local mitigation is to drop a line that repeats across many pages and is shorter than a sentence, and to log every dropped line so a real heading can be put back. The same rule stops a heading from being read twice, once as a chapter title and again as body text. Each source character belongs to one chunk. Overlapping chunk tails, the habit borrowed from search indexing, are what make a sentence play twice.

**Math and code are in the file, and a waveform model will pronounce the markup.** Piper’s API has no Markdown or LaTeX mode. Marker can emit inline math as LaTeX, which is useful in a Markdown file and useless in a speaker: backslashes and command names are what the listener hears. No local TeX-to-speech engine was reviewed. The mitigation that preserves subject matter is to read the surrounding prose and the caption, and to leave a labeled gap (“math omitted,” “code block omitted, N lines,” “figure, no text”) only when the student turns that switch on. The gap is a mutation. It stays off until it is turned on, and the run should say so, because the spoken file then differs from the page. A vision model describing a figure invents subject matter. On Marker’s default LLM path it also sends the image to Gemini. `--use_llm` stays off.

**Citations are part of the sentence, and the engines still mispronounce them.** The default is to speak `(Smith 2020)` in place. pdf2audiobook’s clean stage uses an LLM “to remove citations, tables, figures, and normalize text,” and it inserts an executive summary as the first chapter. That is a subject-matter change. `--llm none` is how that project disables it. The pronunciation evidence that does exist is abbreviations, not a citation corpus: “St.”, `PostgreSQL`, and `PgBouncer` went wrong on both espeak and misaki. nazdridoy has open bugs on Roman numerals, heteronyms, and decimals versus full stops. The mitigation is a pronunciation list and, for Kokoro, an inline IPA override on the tokens that matter. That list is text normalization. It is not a rewrite of the finding. An optional “skip bracketed numeric citations” switch stays off.

**Kokoro’s ONNX voice rejects more than 510 phoneme tokens.** The voice table measured in November 2026 is shaped `(510, 1, 256)`, one style vector per input length. The ONNX README sets a context of 512 and asserts `len(tokens) <= 510` because the ends are pad tokens. The test code raises and tells the caller to split. An NVIDIA ONNX card says the maximum is about 500 tokens and recommends chunks of **100–200**. audio.cpp defaults to **240 characters**, or **100** for Chinese and Japanese, to stay under that phoneme limit. Official `KPipeline` yields audio in a loop for a sample paragraph, and the card does not document the split rules. A page passed as one string can fail or be truncated depending on the runtime. Phoneme tokens are not words: a long sentence can cross 510 even when a short one does not. The mitigation is to split on `.`, `?`, `!`, and paragraph ends, then on `;`, `:`, commas, and spaces, and never inside a word. Count tokens after phonemization, or stay on short sentences. Do not overlap chunks. Piper’s docs, as fetched, state no maximum sentence length. Piper still returns one audio piece per sentence, so the same split is the latency boundary. A PDF run-on with no periods, common when line breaks were the only separator, has to be split on those line boundaries first. ([Kokoro-82M ONNX README](https://huggingface.co/onnx-community/Kokoro-82M-ONNX/blob/6217b562792b37a8638668048dbfc505101c058f/README.md), [Piper vs Kokoro, 4 November 2026](https://bthek1.github.io/DL_tasks/Frameworks/piper_vs_kokoro.html), [audio.cpp Kokoro doc](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/0xShug0/audio.cpp/main/docs/models/kokoro_tts.md))

**VRAM on a 2 GB GPU is not established, for Kokoro or for anyone else in this set.** The 925 MB peak and the 1.1 GB allocation were measured on 16–32 GB cards. Peaks on a large card can differ from peaks on a 2 GB card. The whole-GPU 2.6 GB reading includes memory that was already in use. Hangry’s 2,052 MiB figure is several checkpoints in one process. The mitigation is to leave the GPU idle and run Kokoro, Piper, or pocket-tts on CPU. **24 GB of RAM is not the constraint.** Kokoro’s measured footprints are under 2.5 GB, Piper’s are under 1 GB, and even Qwen’s 5.19 GB CPU figure would fit in RAM while failing the clock. What does threaten RAM is a layout model held for a whole textbook: Docling users have reported spikes up to about **80 GB** on large documents or heavy models, and pypdf documents one uncompressed content stream of about **300 MB** driving about **10 GB** of RAM. Process one page at a time, check huge content streams before parsing, and do not render a page to an image unless that page needs OCR.

**The licences diverge exactly where a student might later share the tool.** Kokoro-82M weights and the hexgrad library are **Apache-2.0**. Piper’s current engine is **GPL-3.0-or-later**, and espeak-ng, which the official Kokoro install also uses, is **GPL-3.0-or-later**. Pocket-tts weights are **CC BY 4.0**. Personal listening is compatible with running all three locally. The GPL becomes a problem when the Piper or espeak binary is linked into an app and shipped; an espeak maintainer has said a closed app should call the unmodified CLI or a separate process, and that a linking exception cannot be obtained because the original author cannot be reached. Piper `lessac` adds a second uncertainty: commercial use of that dataset is unclear, so the public-domain `ljspeech` voice is the one to use if the recording might be shared. KittenTTS’s separate weights file was not read. sherpa-onnx’s licence file was not opened. Marker’s current README says the code is Apache 2.0 and the weights are a modified AI Pubs Open Rail-M, free for research and personal use; OpenDataLoader’s table instead labels Marker GPL-3.0. Both statements are in the notes, and they conflict. PyMuPDF’s own licence file was not opened. ([espeak-ng issue 908](https://github.com/espeak-ng/espeak-ng/issues/908), [Marker README](https://github.com/datalab-to/marker/blob/master/README.md))

**Local speech will sound less finished than a top closed voice.** That is what the boards show, and it is the price of the file staying on disk. Kokoro’s arena neighborhood in October and November 2026 is other open models, with Eleven v3’s short-clip score overlapping Kokoro’s error bar on one snapshot and Eleven v4 sitting far higher on the provider board (Elo **1339** against Kokoro’s **1064**). No source compared these repos, or Kokoro, to NotebookLM Audio Overview with a listening score. The mitigation is to keep the local voice and do the polishing in the player. Listeners commonly play finished audiobooks at **1.5× or 2×**. That is playback of a recording synthesized at the model’s natural rate. Baking Piper’s `length_scale` to something other than 1 forces a resynthesis to change speed. Hosted Kokoro, DashScope, ElevenLabs, and Audio Overview all buy a different product by sending the reading out.

Two further limits belong in the same list. **Line-break hyphens** (“con-” at the end of a line, then “catenate”) are a text-layer habit. Joining a hyphen only at a line end can also destroy a real compound such as “self-report.” No library flag that does only the safe join was confirmed. The repair writes a sidecar and stays off until the student opts in. **DRM** is outside these extractors. make-audiobook refuses Kindle AZW and Adobe DRM EPUB. A standard PDF password can be supplied to pdfminer or pypdf. A copy-protected file that still rasterizes can be OCR’d, and the OCR text will be worse than a text layer. Scanned pages are also the path that can miss the time budget: OCR seconds per page on this machine were not measured.

## Extract the file, chunk on sentences, and time one kilobyte

The work is a local command pipeline. After voices and, if used, layout weights are on disk, the read path does not need a network. The steps below use only calls and flags the notes document.

**Install the CPU stack and leave the GPU idle.** Install system espeak-ng (the official Kokoro card installs it; whether a distro package in November 2026 matches release 1.52 was not checked). Use a Python that the Kokoro wrappers still support: PierrunoYT documents **3.10–3.12** and says **3.13+** fails because misaki has no 3.13 packages in their account; nazdridoy documents **3.11–3.12**. Upstream’s own version bound is not separately stated. Then `pip install kokoro`. Download the v1.0 weights from `hexgrad/Kokoro-82M` once. Install ffmpeg for the final mux. For the speed fallback, `pip install piper-tts` and download one ONNX voice, `en_US-lessac-medium` if private-use speed is the goal, or `en_US-ljspeech-high` if the public-domain voice is the goal. If Docling is reserved for bad column files, run `docling-tools models download` and point `artifacts_path` at that directory. `enable_remote_services` stays at its default, false. Do not start Marker’s Gemini helper, and do not set `--use_llm`.

**Extract text with no model in the loop.** A `.txt` file is already the reading. A `.md` file is read as text; the speech rules below strip syntax and keep the words. A born-digital `.pdf` is read one page at a time with PyMuPDF `page.get_text(sort=True)` or with pdfminer.six `extract_text` under default `LAParams` (`boxes_flow` default **0.5**). Keep the page’s word boxes so a bad page can be retried without redoing the book. `pymupdf4llm.to_markdown(..., page_chunks=True)` is the variant that preserves Markdown headings, at a quality cost the **14 November 2026** reader board still shows: pymupdf4llm 2.1 passed **43.4%** of that board’s checks, Docling 2.150.0 passed **64.2%**, and Marker’s row was marked unfair because its OCR failed on 93 pages. Scanned pages use PyMuPDF `page.get_textpage_ocr()` or Docling with Tesseract on CPU. EasyOCR-on-GPU does not fit in 2 GB. Time any OCR path on its own. It was not signed off against the 30-second budget.

**Normalize Markdown and PDF with rules, and leave every mutation off.** Piper and Kokoro speak a plain string. The default that keeps the subject matter is to read the words and skip the markup characters.

| Construct | Spoken by default | Left to an opt-in switch |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Headings | The heading words, then a short pause. The same words become the chapter title | The hash marks, and the word “heading” |
| Emphasis | The words inside `*` or `_` | The asterisks and underscores |
| Paragraphs | One pause between paragraphs | A pause for every blank line |
| Lists | The item text, in order, including a real step number | The bullet glyph |
| Citations | The citation text, in place | Stripping bracketed citations. Off, because it changes the sentence |
| Code fences | The fence’s words, if the switch is left off. Backticks are still not words | “Code block omitted, N lines.” On only when the student accepts a gap |
| Tables | Caption and a row-wise reading, pipe characters skipped | “Table omitted, M rows” |
| LaTeX | Nothing reliable exists in the notes. Speaking `\frac` pronounces the command | A labeled “math omitted” gap. Off until opted in |
| URLs | The anchor text, or the domain when the URL is the whole text | Replacing the URL with the word “link” |
| Images | The caption. If there is no caption and no alt text, there are no source words | A model-written description. Off. It invents the figure |
| Line-end hyphens | Kept as stored | A sidecar that joins a hyphen only at a line end. Off, because “self-report” is a real compound |
| LLM cleanup | Off. This includes pdf2audiobook’s default cleaner, Marker `--use_llm`, local Ollama, and Libratory’s summary buttons | On only as an explicit mutation. A local model can still substitute words |

This table is an operating rule inferred from how these engines consume strings, and from pdf2audiobook’s documented cleaner. It is not a published university reading standard. No screen-reader Markdown spec was opened.

**Chunk, then synthesize at the model’s natural rate.** Split on sentence boundaries and paragraph ends. If a Kokoro piece would exceed **510 phoneme tokens**, split on semicolon, colon, comma, then spaces. Never split inside a word, and never overlap the next chunk. Drop whitespace-only pieces. Collapse blank lines to a single paragraph pause. Concatenate PCM with no extra gap except that pause. Empty chunks and a pause token on every newline are where long silences come from. The official pipeline returns more than one piece for a paragraph; one `kokoro-onnx` stream call, by contrast, returned a whole utterance as a single chunk and took **9.6 seconds** to first audio on a 321-character paragraph on one i9 thread. Sentence calls are what that test says bring first audio down. Piper’s maximum length is not stated; sentence splits match the unit it returns.

**Write a chaptered M4B, and keep MP3 as the player fallback.** Synthesize to PCM or WAV at the model’s rate. Kokoro’s card writes 24 kHz WAV. Build one chapter per heading. Body text before the first heading is a chapter named from the filename. Timestamps are the cumulative PCM length at each heading, in milliseconds. ffmpeg’s chapter file, as used by audiobook tools, is an `FFMETADATA` document: a `;FFMETADATA1` header and one `[CHAPTER]` block with `TIMEBASE=1/1000`, `START`, `END`, and `title`, muxed into an `.m4b` with stream copy so the audio is encoded once. MP3 is the fallback when a player ignores M4B; chapter support there is less consistent. Opus is a reasonable archive copy and a weak phone-chapter story; phone chapter behavior was not measured. Set speed in the player. Leave synthesis speed at the model default.

**Confirm the speed bar on this machine, on about one kilobyte of extracted plain text.** The pass mark is under **30 seconds** of processing for that kilobyte, synthesis included, extraction separated so a slow PDF is not blamed on the voice. Using the April 2025 card average, a thousand characters are about a minute of audio, and the bar is about real-time factor **0.5**. Using **155 words per minute** and **170–205 words per kilobyte** of ASCII English, the same 30 seconds is about real-time factor **0.38** on the denser kilobyte and **0.45** on the sparser one. Words per kilobyte were not measured on the student’s own notes, and the ACX “about 9,300 words per finished hour” figure those rates lean on is second-hand in the notes. Published Kokoro fp32 on four threads was **0.172** (i9, 5 November 2026) and **0.267** (four vCPU, 4 November 2026). One pinned i9 thread was **0.517**. If the student’s timed kilobyte takes more than 30 seconds, switch that run to Piper medium. Do not treat tts-bench’s **14.38×** Kokoro number as a promise: clip length and CPU thread count are not stated. Do not promise the published **0.054** Piper figure on this PC either. Those runs were short, warm clips on someone else’s desktop.

A planning sketch already in the notes shows the shape of a born-digital pack. At about **2 KB** of text per page, **300 pages** are about **600 KB**. At the strict cap that pack may take up to **5 hours**. Piper medium at real-time factor **0.054**, and about **79 seconds** of audio per dense kilobyte, is about **43 minutes** of synthesis plus cheap text-layer extraction. Kokoro at **0.267** uses a larger share of the same 30-second allowance and, on the four-thread machines that were tested, still finishes a kilobyte inside it. Text-layer extraction is small next to 30 seconds on other people’s hardware: OpenDataLoader lists pymupdf4llm at **0.091 seconds per page** and Docling at **0.762**. Marker at **53.9 seconds per page** on that table would consume the budget by itself, and Marker’s own release notes claim multiple pages per second. The two Marker descriptions conflict, so Marker stays off the default path until `--disable_ocr` or CPU fast is timed here. A scanned pack stays unbounded until OCR seconds per page are measured. Encode the finished PCM once. Encoding every sentence as its own MP3 process is the variant that adds spawn overhead across thousands of files. Stream the PCM to disk. A 16-bit mono **22.05 kHz** Piper stream is about **2.5 MB per minute**, about **1.5 GB** for 10 hours, which fits on disk and should not be held twice in RAM next to a parser.

## Audio Overview discusses the sources; this pipeline reads them

Google’s Gemini Notebook help describes Audio Overviews as “deep-dive discussions between AI hosts that provide in-depth summaries of the key topics in your uploaded sources.” The formats it lists are Deep Dive, with two hosts; The Brief, one speaker and key takeaways in under two minutes; The Critique; and The Debate. Length can be Shorter, Default, or Longer, English only. A prompt can steer topic and expertise. The same page says overviews may contain inaccuracies or audio glitches, and that generation can take a couple of minutes. Interactive mode, English only, lets the listener join and ask questions. The launch post says the hosts “summarize your material, make connections between topics, and banter,” and that the discussions “are not a comprehensive or objective view of a topic, but simply a reflection of the sources.” A secondary walkthrough, using the name Gemini Notebook for the product since July 2026, says there is no read-aloud of the source documents and that tables and formulas get simplified or skipped. That walkthrough is not Google’s documentation. The help page fetched for these notes does not contain the sentence “this is not verbatim.” The contrast is in the format names, in the launch post’s own description, and on the secondary pages. ([Gemini Notebook help](https://support.google.com/gemininotebook/answer/16212820?hl=en), [NotebookLM Audio Overviews launch post](https://blog.google/innovation-and-ai/products/notebooklm-audio-overviews/))

The local pipeline does the inverse. It speaks extracted text, in order, after a deterministic pass that drops markup characters and, only if the student opts in, drops code, math, or a repeated header under a label. It does not add a host, a brief, a critique, a debate, or an executive summary. It does not upload the file unless someone turns on DashScope, Marker’s Gemini helper, Unstructured’s remote URL, Docling’s remote picture descriptions, or a hosted Kokoro endpoint. Generation time for Audio Overview is a couple of minutes for a discussion. Generation time here is the synthesis budget, under 30 seconds per kilobyte on the engines that cleared it, for a reading that is as long as the source. The local voice will be less polished. The words will be the words that were on the page, mispronounced where the phonemizer is weak, and present where a summary would have dropped them.

## Conclusion

The 2 GB GPU is the part of this machine that does not decide the outcome. Kokoro, Piper, and pocket-tts already fit in system RAM, and the only neural models that would make the card feel necessary measured either too large or too slow. Leaving the GPU idle is what makes the speed bar and the privacy bar true together. What decides whether the audio matches the course is the extractor: text-layer pages for born-digital files, a layout model only after a column failure, OCR only for pages that have no text, and every LLM cleanup switch left off because it changes the text. The remaining uncertainty is the student’s own core count. Four threads of Kokoro cleared both readings of the bar on the desktops timed in November 2026. One pinned i9 thread, at real-time factor **0.517**, finished a minute of audio in about **31 seconds** and missed the 30-second line. A single timed kilobyte on this PC tells those results apart, and Piper medium is already the documented way to land on the fast side of that line.
